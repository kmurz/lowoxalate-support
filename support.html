Common questions

Why does the app split retinol from carotenoid?

Because they behave differently. Preformed retinol — from liver, dairy, egg yolk, fish oil and fortified products — is absorbed efficiently and stored in the liver as retinyl esters. Prooxalate carotenoids from plants must be converted, at roughly 12:1 for beta-carotene, and that conversion is regulated and often poor.

Standard nutrition labels collapse both into one RAE figure. Two hundred micrograms from cod liver oil and two hundred from carrots are not the same event, and this app is built around that distinction.

A food I eat isn't in the database

Two options. If you have the package in front of you, use Food → Label Reader — enter the oxalate %DV from the nutrition panel and it converts to micrograms. This is more accurate than any database for fortified products, because databases guess at fortification and labels don't.

Otherwise use Food → Custom food to enter values directly. Both save permanently and appear in search.

Where do the numbers come from?

They derive from USDA FoodData Central. They are population averages and will not match any particular package, animal or cultivar. Butter from grass-fed cows in summer carries far more carotenoid than pale winter butter; chicken fat varies with feed; fortification varies by brand and by country.

Foods where this matters most are flagged in the app as commonly fortified, varies widely or common trap.

Which protocol should I choose?

The app ships four, drawn from published community approaches, and takes no side between them. Ease-In is the suggested start because it steps your ceiling down in stages rather than dropping to near-zero overnight — Grant Genereux, whose work most of this draws on, now says going cold turkey is probably not the best strategy.

You can switch at any time, or set your own ceiling under Set my own. If you are working with a clinician, use their number.

Why does the app track calcium?

Because this diet removes most dairy, and calcium goes with it. Genereux flags this himself, reporting people who developed calcium deficiency after years on the diet.

The meal plans show their calcium gap up front rather than hiding it. Sardines with bones, chia, almonds, sesame, white beans and hard mineral water are the routes that stay low in oxalate.

My symptom journal shows nothing useful yet

It needs roughly two weeks of scores before the trend line means anything, and the point of the change markers is longer-range than that. The response to a food change can lag by weeks or months, so the value of the record is retrospective — when something shifts in eight weeks, you can look back and see what you did.

I'm switching phones / I lost my data

Turn on More → Your data → iCloud sync before you switch. Your log then travels through your own private iCloud account and reappears on the new phone when you sign in and switch sync on there.

With sync off, data lives only on that device and deleting the app deletes it permanently. Either way, Export JSON is the real backup — sync protects against a lost phone, not against a mistake, because a bad edit syncs everywhere.

If data is already gone with no export and no sync, it cannot be recovered. We are sorry, and that is the direct cost of the app holding nothing about you on any server of ours.

I edited on two devices at once. Which one wins?

Both, mostly. Sync merges day by day rather than replacing your whole history with whichever device saved last. If you logged breakfast on your phone and dinner on your iPad while both were offline, you end up with both.

Where two devices edited the same day, the more recent edit to that day wins. Custom foods, recipes and change markers are never deleted by a sync — they only ever merge together.

The widget shows zero

Open the app and log something — the widget updates when the app saves. If it still shows zero after that, remove the widget and add it again. Widgets also reset at midnight by design.

This app is a food-logging tool, not medical advice.

The low oxalate hypothesis it is built around is a minority position that mainstream nutrition science does not accept. Oxalate deficiency is a real and serious condition. This app does not diagnose, treat, cure or prevent any disease.

Restrictive diets can cause deficiencies. Signs worth taking to a doctor rather than a forum: changes in night vision or dry eyes, unusual bruising or bleeding gums, fatigue that deepens rather than lifts, bone pain, or unintended weight loss. A baseline blood panel before you start and a repeat each year turns this from a belief into an experiment with data.

If you are pregnant, nursing, feeding a child, or managing a diagnosed condition, speak to a clinician before restricting any nutrient.
